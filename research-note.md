# Pushback, not throughput: what good agentic practice is for

Much practitioner writing on agentic coding measures success in output: more products per engineer, more agents running at once. I argue throughput measures efficiency, not whether agentic practice is good. Good practice should be judged by how strongly it pushes back on wrong output, and how much understanding the developer keeps. Harnesses are necessary for that, but not sufficient: they move judgement to a different layer rather than replace it.

## Throughput claims rest on weak evidence

The strongest throughput claims come from people building products around it. Klaassen's [*Compound Engineering*](https://every.to/guides/compound-engineering) guide for Every describes single-person teams running several products and claims skipping permission prompts "will unlock five to 10 times faster iteration". None of this is measured. Its "95 percent garbage rate" for first attempts is reported from experience, and its claim the approach "will become the default way software is built" is a prediction. Every sells AI products, so the account is also an advertisement.

Where throughput has been measured, it looks different. Willison's discussion of METR's randomised trial of sixteen experienced open-source developers shows tasks took about 19% longer with AI allowed, while the developers believed they had been sped up by about 20% ([Willison, 2025](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Jul/12/ai-open-source-productivity/)). The study is small and used early-2025 tools, and Willison questions whether a learning curve explains it. METR's [2026 follow-up](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/) suggests newer tools may help, though selection effects make the size uncertain. Either way, the earlier study shows developers' perception of their own speed can be unreliable, so self-reported throughput is poor evidence a practice is good.

## Good practice has moved into the harness

As code becomes cheaper to produce, the challenge shifts to specifying and verifying what gets built. Willison puts it plainly: writing code is cheap now, and the engineer's job is to specify problems at the right level of detail and verify the results "until we are confident" ([Willison, Agentic Engineering Patterns](https://simonwillison.net/guides/agentic-engineering-patterns/what-is-agentic-engineering)). Böckeler gives this a structure ([2026](https://martinfowler.com/articles/harness-engineering.html)): a harness has guides that steer the agent before it acts and sensors that check its work after, each either computational, like tests and type checkers, or inferential, like an LLM reviewer. On this view, good practice is the quality of the pushback.

Yet Böckeler's own account marks where the harness stops. She calls verifying behaviour "the elephant in the room", calls AI-generated tests "not good enough yet", and notes sensors can't judge correctness when intent isn't stated clearly. The literature arguing most strongly for harnesses also concedes they depend on a human who knows what the software is for.

## What the harness cannot see

Storey's account of cognitive and intent debt names what is lost when that human stops understanding ([Storey, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22106)). Agents can reduce visible technical debt while a team's shared mental model and recorded rationale erode. The paper is conceptual, not empirical: a vocabulary, not proof. The METR result shows developers can misjudge their own speed; Storey's framework raises a further concern, that they may also fail to notice their understanding of the system weakening. Strathern's account of Goodhart's law ([1997](https://gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/1997-strathern.pdf)) adds that once a check becomes the target, passing it may stop reflecting what it was meant to measure. A harness tuned only to turn checks green can drift from the intent those checks stood for.

## The counter-case

The strongest counter comes from compound engineering itself. Klaassen lists "every line must be manually reviewed" as a belief to unlearn: if you don't trust the output, fix the system rather than reading everything. This is partly right: mechanical review can be delegated to sensors, and inspecting every generated line limits a harness's benefit. But the same guide still tells readers to review the plan and the pull request, and admits that for beginners "the learning happens when you review every line". The disagreement is about where judgement sits, not whether it's needed. That concession is my argument: the harness moves judgement upward, to intent and design, and practice is good to the extent it keeps someone able to exercise it there.

## Reflection is the work that remains

Schön described professional practice as a reflective conversation with the situation, in which the practitioner acts, sees how it talks back, and revises ([*The Reflective Practitioner: How Professionals Think in Action*](https://archive.org/details/reflectivepracti0000scho), Basic Books, 1983). Agents compress the acting. What remains is the noticing: recognising that a passing test suite and a stalled user are different results. In my own project, tests were green while a classmate could not work out what to do after joining a circle; no sensor I had written could have detected that. Good agentic practice keeps that conversation going: the agent produces the changes, but the developer stays responsible for interpreting what happens and deciding what to change next.

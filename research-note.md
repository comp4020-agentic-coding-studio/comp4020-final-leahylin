# Pushback, not throughput: what good agentic practice is for

Much practitioner writing on agentic coding measures success in output: more products per engineer, more agents running at once. I argue that throughput measures efficiency, but it is insufficient to judge whether agentic practice is good. Good agentic practice should be judged by how strongly it pushes back on wrong output and by how much understanding the developer keeps. Harnesses are necessary for that, but not sufficient: they move human judgement to a different layer rather than replacing it.

## Throughput claims rest on weak evidence

The strongest statements of the throughput view come from people building products around it. Shipper and Klaassen's account of "compound engineering" in Every's [*Compounding Engineering*](https://every.to/c/compounding-engineering) series ([2025](https://every.to/chain-of-thought/compound-engineering-how-every-codes-with-agents)) describes single engineers running several products and claims that skipping permission prompts "will unlock five to 10 times faster iteration". None of this is measured. Their "95 percent garbage rate" for first attempts is reported from experience, and their claim that the approach will become the default is a prediction. Every sells AI products, so the account is also an advertisement.

Where throughput has been measured, it looks different. Willison's discussion of METR's randomised trial of sixteen experienced open-source developers shows that tasks took about 19% longer with AI allowed, while the developers believed they had been sped up by about 20% ([Willison, 2025](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Jul/12/ai-open-source-productivity/)). The study is small and used early-2025 tools, and Willison questions whether a learning curve explains it. METR's [2026 follow-up](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/) suggests that newer tools may improve productivity, although selection effects make the size of any improvement uncertain. The ealier study still shows that developers' perception of their own speed can be unreliable. Self-reported throughput is therefore poor evidence that a practice is good.

## Good practice has moved into the harness

As code becomes cheaper to produce, the challenge shifts towards specifying and verifying what gets built. Willison puts it plainly: writing code is cheap now, and the engineer's job is to specify problems at the right level of detail and verify the results "until we are confident" ([Willison, Agentic Engineering Patterns](https://simonwillison.net/guides/agentic-engineering-patterns/what-is-agentic-engineering)). Böckeler gives this a structure ([2026](https://martinfowler.com/articles/harness-engineering.html)). A harness has guides that steer the agent before it acts and sensors that check its work afterwards, and each can be computational, like tests and type checkers, or inferential, like an LLM reviewer. On this view, good practice is the quality of the pushback.

Yet Böckeler's own account marks where the harness stops. She calls verifying behaviour "the elephant in the room", says that relying on AI-generated tests is "not good enough yet", and notes that sensors cannot judge correctness when the human has not stated the intent clearly. The literature that argues most strongly for harnesses also concedes that they depend on a human who knows what the software is for.

## What the harness cannot see

Storey's account of cognitive and intent debt names what is lost when that human stops understanding ([Storey, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22106)). Agents can reduce visible technical debt while a team's shared mental model and its recorded rationale erode. Storey's paper is conceptual rather than empirical, so it offers a vocabulary, not proof. The METR result shows that developers can misjudge their own speed. Storey's framework raises a further concern: they may also fail to notice when their understanding of the system is weakening. Strathern's version of Goodhart's law adds that once a check becomes the target, passing it stops meaning what it used to. A harness tuned only to turn checks green can drift away from the intent those checks stood for.

## The counter-case

The strongest objection comes from compound engineering itself. Shipper and Klaassen list "every line must be manually reviewed" as a belief to unlearn: if you do not trust the output, fix the system rather than reading everything. This is partly right. Mechanical review can be delegated to sensors, and requiring developers to inspect every generated line limits the benefits of a harness. But the same guide still tells readers to review the plan and the pull request, and admits that for beginners "the learning happens when you review every line". The disagreement is about where human judgement sits, not whether it is needed. That concession is my argument: the harness moves judgement upward, to intent and design, and practice is good to the extent that it keeps a person able to exercise it there.

## Reflection is the work that remains

Schön described professional practice as a reflective conversation with the situation, in which the practitioner acts, sees how the situation talks back, and revises ([*The Reflective Practitioner: How Professionals Think in Action*](https://archive.org/details/reflectivepracti0000scho), Basic Books, 1983). Agents compress the acting. What remains is the noticing: recognising that a passing test suite and a stalled user are different results. In my own project, tests were green while a classmate could not work out what to do after joining a circle; no sensor I had written could have detected that. Good agentic practice keeps that conversation going: the agent produces the changes, but the developer remains responsible for interpreting what happens and deciding what needs to change next.

# Process overview

*Final version.*

## From the brief to the harness

The brief asks for a multi-user, real-time app that's good. I chose an **activity buddy finder** for ANU students: a way to find someone you know to run, play badminton, get coffee or try something else with this week.

I used the agent to explore what might help people move from agreeing to meet to actually turning up. This shaped my definition of **good**: concrete, nearby meetups; one action to join or leave; an accurate head count; and edits that say exactly what changed.

I connected that definition to three documents. `README.md` explains the product decisions ([`9a64a3e`](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-leahylin/commit/9a64a3e)), `CLAUDE.md` turns each commitment into a rule for the agent ([`7fba852`](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-leahylin/commit/7fba852)), and `spec/` tests those rules through HTTP requests to the running app ([`ce1f84b`](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-leahylin/commit/ce1f84b)). For the final, `spec/README.md` maps README claims to the tests that enforce them (100, all passing) and records how the judged questions were assessed ([`d68b656`](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-leahylin/commit/d68b656)).

## How good changed

**Crit 8 → 9: only people you know.** A pod member pointed out that strangers could see a meetup's time and place and turn up without knowing the organiser or attendees, creating a safety risk. My assumption that a low-friction app should not require accounts no longer held once I considered who could show up. I changed the harness before writing code, rather than treating accounts and visibility checks as an unstructured bundle of features ([`095dfdf`](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-leahylin/commit/095dfdf), [decision 4](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-leahylin/blob/main/docs/decisions/0004-accounts-circles-friends.md)). I then guided implementation through four tested steps: accounts ([`f99164a`](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-leahylin/commit/f99164a)), circles capped at 25 members ([`a8e8f24`](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-leahylin/commit/a8e8f24)), direct friendships ([`6138339`](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-leahylin/commit/6138339)), and meetup visibility ([`3765a4d`](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-leahylin/commit/3765a4d)). The change was not just adding login: access rules became part of the design and were tested as the app grew.

**Crit 9: using it myself.** Live updates use a simple “something changed” signal, with each page fetching its own data ([decision 3](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-leahylin/blob/main/docs/decisions/0003-live-updates.md)). I tested the actual user flow rather than relying on route tests alone. This exposed two problems: friend requests didn't appear live ([`fbeff10`](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-leahylin/commit/fbeff10)), and circles were difficult to reach on a phone ([`aa946c6`](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-leahylin/commit/aa946c6)). I directed the agent to trace the update flow and improve mobile navigation, then tested the changes in the running app.

**Crit 10: readable from the outside.** A presenter had to explain the app using only `flyctl logs`, but Buddy Up initially had just one startup line. I directed the agent to log each request as a `key=value` line recording who did what and how it ended ([decision 6](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-leahylin/blob/main/docs/decisions/0006-action-logging.md), [multi-user test report](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-leahylin/blob/main/docs/testing/multi-user-test-report.md)). Reading the logs as a presenter exposed another problem: invite links appeared in full on the projector. I had them masked ([`7daa380`](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-leahylin/commit/7daa380)).

**Final: people, not tests.** I gave three classmates a URL and a circle code and asked them to join a coffee meetup without help. Two finished in 45 and 60 seconds. The third was still unsure what to do after joining the circle at the 90-second mark. They rated how easy the process felt from 1 (effortless) to 5 (a chore), giving scores of 3, 4 and 2. The circle page showed members and invite tools but not upcoming meetups, which may have caused the confusion. I directed the agent to show the circle's meetups ([`535d421`](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-leahylin/commit/535d421), [`52bb3b4`](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-leahylin/commit/52bb3b4)). A new classmate then completed the full flow in 50 seconds and rated it 1. This suggests the change may have helped, but one retest cannot show that joining is easy for everyone.

By the final, I understood good as more than a working meetup flow: it also meant controlling access, making behaviour observable, and checking usability with real people.

## Agentic workflow

Working with Claude taught me that clear instructions and passing tests do not replace human judgement. At Crit 8, Claude built and committed the entire app in one go without my approval. I reverted those changes and directed Claude to rebuild the app in smaller, reviewable steps ([`f38ad35...d5a90d2`](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-leahylin/compare/f38ad35...d5a90d2)). I also updated [`CLAUDE.md`](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-leahylin/blob/main/CLAUDE.md) to require my approval before commits. This experience showed me that Claude could implement fixes, but I still had to judge which problems mattered and whether the fixes worked ([`3f25665`](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-leahylin/commit/3f25665)). Reviewing each change took more time than accepting Claude's output automatically, but it meant I retained control over the decisions and responsibility for the final result.

## The readings

Robin Sloan's [home-cooked app](https://www.robinsloan.com/notes/home-cooked-app/) is made for a few people who already share a table. I agree that small software can have its own standard of good, and for Buddy Up, that means meetups happening rather than growth. However, Sloan's app needs no accounts because its users are his family. Crit 8 showed me that when people outside an existing circle might join, trust needs to be considered in the design ([decision 4](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-leahylin/blob/main/docs/decisions/0004-accounts-circles-friends.md)).

Clay Shirky's [situated software](https://gwern.net/doc/technology/2004-03-30-shirky-situatedsoftware.html) is shaped by the group it serves. Buddy Up is designed for ANU students, with Canberra time, nearby places, a seven-day horizon, and small circles. I agree with designing for a specific community rather than everyone, but fitting a context does not prove that a product works. Whether meetups actually happen depends on people using Buddy Up over time, something that testing during crits could not establish ([`spec/README.md`](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-leahylin/blob/main/spec/README.md#judged-readmes-questions-for-people-and-what-came-of-them)).

Darius Kazemi [argues](https://runyourown.social/) that keeping a social space small is a deliberate choice, not a failure to scale. I agree, and Buddy Up's 25-member cap, with no public meetups or friends of friends, reflects that choice ([`a8e8f24`](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-leahylin/commit/a8e8f24)). Kazemi emphasises community moderation, while I place more emphasis on techical controls, checking visibility on every read and write([`3765a4d`](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-leahylin/commit/3765a4d)) and logging denied actions ([`7daa380`](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-leahylin/commit/7daa380)). These controls reduce reliance on constant moderation, but cannot guarantee that every interaction is safe.

## Stack

I chose Node 24 to run TypeScript directly, `node:sqlite` for data storage on a Fly volume, and server-rendered forms that work without JavaScript ([`d5a90d2`](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-leahylin/commit/d5a90d2)). This kept dependencies and deployment simple on a 256 MB machine. SQLite transactions also help prevent two users from claiming the last available spot at the same time ([`ce1f84b`](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-leahylin/commit/ce1f84b), [`d411b7b`](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-leahylin/commit/d411b7b)).

The trade-off is that this design suits a small, single-machine app rather than horizontal scaling. SQLite on one Fly volume simplifies data management, but adding machines would require changes to shared storage and live updates ([`19ac552`](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-leahylin/commit/19ac552), [`d411b7b`](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-leahylin/commit/d411b7b)). I accepted this limitation because Buddy Up serves one campus, where simplicity and inspectability mattered more than scaling to a large user base.
